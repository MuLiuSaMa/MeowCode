# Visual Planning

Use this planning pass before drawing. It is an internal design step, not a request to deliver an incomplete wireframe.

## Define the Reader Outcome

Write one sentence describing what the reader should understand, compare, decide, or remember after scanning the canvas. Use it to decide what deserves the most space and contrast.

## Classify Information by Role

Classify the material independently of its source format:

- core claim or conclusion;
- evidence, examples, or details;
- relationships or dependencies;
- context, constraints, or background;
- reference, index, or taxonomy;
- actions, prompts, or open questions.

An item being numbered, ordered, or titled does not automatically make it a node in a flow.

## Cluster by Cognitive Purpose

Place information together when readers need it for the same mental task. Separate content that serves different purposes even if it appeared together in the source.

Useful generic clusters include orientation and summary, explanation and evidence, comparison and trade-offs, relationships and dependencies, examples and application, and next actions or discussion.

## Select a Visual Grammar

Choose the simplest grammar that matches the meaning:

- comparison: aligned columns, scales, or paired regions;
- spatial cluster: proximity and shared alignment;
- relationship network: nodes and semantic connectors;
- sequence: ordered steps only when progression matters;
- hierarchy: nested levels or branches;
- matrix: two explicit dimensions;
- index or reference: compact list, legend, or table-like region.

Different clusters may use different grammars on one canvas. Do not force the whole document into one graph.

## Translate Data Into Visual Evidence

Treat spreadsheets, KPI lists, and activity reports as evidence rather than as a ready-made layout. Select the visual form from the question the reader needs to answer:

- use a prominent number and short claim for one headline result;
- use aligned values, progress, or a shared baseline for target-versus-actual comparisons;
- use bars for ranking, magnitude, or contribution differences;
- use a compact table or matrix when readers need exact values across several comparable entities;
- use spatially aligned before/after or period comparisons for change;
- place conflicting totals side by side when the inconsistency itself is the finding;
- express actions as action, evidence, and intended outcome rather than as undifferentiated prose.

Do not copy worksheet order, simulate a table with repeated `|` or `｜` characters, or turn every metric into an equal card. Combine visual forms when the material requires different reading tasks, but do not force a fixed dashboard template.

## Plan the Reading Path

Decide the entry point, primary focal region, second and third stops, and where the reader exits into actions, questions, or references.

Use position, scale, contrast, and whitespace to express this path. Connector lines should encode relationships, not compensate for weak layout.

## Move from Frame to Complete Whiteboard

Create a rough internal frame to establish regions and proportions. Then populate it with the full requested information. Deliver the rough frame only when the user explicitly asks for a sketch, wireframe, or layout proposal.
